# Accessible: the intended reader gets it on one read

Read this when the reader is outside the writer's field, reads in a second language, or
is skimming. Accessible doesn't mean simple-minded: an expert reading outside their
specialty is the usual case.

## Words

- Use the word the reader would use. "Sign-in" for the public, "authentication" for
  engineers. Write the reader's term even when yours is more precise, then be precise.
- Define a term the first time it carries weight, in the same sentence, or avoid it.
  Spell out an acronym once unless every reader already says it (PDF, USB).
- One name per thing. Switching between "the account", "the profile" and "the user
  record" makes readers wonder if there are three things.
- Turn nouns back into verbs: "make a decision" is "decide", "perform an analysis of"
  is "analyse", "provide assistance" is "help".
- Don't translate an expert's daily vocabulary into plain speech for them. When a
  domain reader finds something unclear, the cause is almost always an unexplained
  number or an internal codename, not the jargon.

## Sentences

- Put the subject and the verb early. Long openers ("Given the constraints imposed by
  the current infrastructure, …") make the reader hold everything before they know
  what it's for.
- One idea per sentence for general readers. Most sentences under 20 words, a few
  longer when the idea needs it; past 30, split it.
- Active voice when the actor matters: "The vendor cancelled the contract", not "The
  contract was cancelled".
- Positive statements: "Keep the lid on" beats "Don't leave the lid off", and double
  negatives ("not uncommon") cost a second read.
- For readers in a second language, avoid idioms ("ballpark", "move the needle"),
  phrasal verbs with several meanings ("run into", "put off"), sarcasm and culture-bound
  references. They reach native readers too.

## Numbers

- Give the base with every rate: "12 of 400 orders (3%)", not "3% of orders".
- Compare to something the reader knows: "about the weight of a laptop", "two working
  days".
- Round to what the decision needs. "$1.24M" for a budget line, "about $1.2 million"
  in a sentence.
- Put the comparison in the sentence, not only in a chart.

## Structure

- Answer first; reasons after. Readers who stop early still leave with the point.
- Short paragraphs, but connected: each picks up something the last one introduced.
- Lists for steps and for parallel items a reader will scan; prose when each point
  depends on the one before.
- Headings that say what the section concludes ("Billing is blocked by the vendor"),
  not what it contains ("Billing").
- Link text that says where it goes ("the refund policy"), never "click here".

## For readers using assistive technology

- Headings in order, one level at a time, so a screen reader's outline makes sense.
- Alt text that says what an image shows and why it's there; empty alt text for
  decoration.
- Never use colour alone to carry meaning; say it in text too.
- Tables with a header row, and no merged cells where a list would do.
