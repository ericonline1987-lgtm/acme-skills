# Decks

A deck is an argument the audience follows one claim at a time while someone talks.
Read this when building or fixing slides, whether presented live or sent to be read.

## Decide which kind of deck it is

| | Presented live | Read alone (sent ahead, left behind) |
|---|---|---|
| Words on a slide | A headline and the proving visual; the talk carries the rest | Full sentences; the slide carries the argument |
| Density | One idea, readable from the back of the room | Up to a dense half-page, still one idea |
| Speaker notes | What to say, not what's on the slide | Optional |
| Typical failure | A document pasted onto slides | Fragments that only make sense with a speaker |

If it must do both, build it for reading and give the presenter a short version, or
put the full text in the notes; a slide can't be both.

## Build the storyline before any slide

1. Write the outcome: who is in the room, what they believe now, what they should
   believe or do at the end, and the time slot.
2. Write the headlines only, as full takeaway sentences ("Churn fell 12% after the
   onboarding change"), never topic labels ("Churn"). Read them in order: they should
   make the whole argument in under a minute. If they don't, no visual will fix it.
3. List the three hardest questions this audience will ask, and make sure a headline or
   an appendix slide answers each.
4. Close with the ask written out as the last headline: what is to be decided or done,
   with amount, owner, timeframe and how success will be judged. A slide titled "The
   ask" is not an ask. Case: in this set's evals, funding decks closed on a slide titled
   "The ask" whose bullets described what an ask should contain; the fix was a headline
   the room could approve: "Fund two engineers for six months; we report build time
   monthly".

## Each slide

- One claim, stated in the headline; beneath it, the one visual or at most three short
  lines that prove it. The audience reads the headline, then looks for the proof.
- Show before telling: the screenshot, the real number, the before-and-after, not a
  sentence asserting it. Keep captured evidence raw (crop, don't redraw); a recreated
  screenshot reads as fabricated.
- Every number carries its basis on the slide (source, period, n).
- A before-and-after pair differs only in the thing claimed: same crop, theme, data and
  size. Otherwise the audience sees the incidental difference.
- Mark what's missing as missing (`[Q3 figure from finance]`); never mock up a chart to
  fill the gap.

## Type, layout and colour for a room

- Body text at least 24 pt on a 16:9 slide, headlines larger; nothing under 18 pt,
  including chart labels and footnotes. If it doesn't fit at that size, it's two slides.
- A consistent grid: the headline in the same place on every slide, generous margins,
  content filling the area rather than floating in a corner.
- One accent colour, spent on the element the headline names; everything else neutral.
  Colour that carries meaning also gets a label or shape.
- Dark rooms: avoid full-white slides; use a dark theme or mid-tone background and
  check every element for contrast against it.
- Motion only to pace a dense slide (reveal one layer at a time) or walk through a
  process; never to decorate.

## Timing

About one to two minutes per content slide. Rehearse against the clock; move what
doesn't fit into a clearly optional appendix ("open any of these if asked"), don't
squeeze it into smaller type.

## Before handing it over

- Headlines alone tell the story; the last one is the ask.
- Every slide checked at real size, full screen, in the venue's aspect ratio: no
  overflow, nothing cut off, legible from the back (see `rendered-checks.md`).
- The deck and any document it came from say the same thing; change both together.
