# Briefing decisions

Follow `../../shared/style-guide.md` for voice.

A brief is judged by the decision it produces, not by how much it covers. The reader
should be able to decide from the first screen and check the reasoning below it.

## Process

1. **Name the decision, the decider and the deadline.** One sentence: "Choose a payment
   provider for EU launch; Dana decides by 30 Oct." If the decider is unclear, that is
   the first thing to resolve.
2. **State what success looks like** for the decision, in the decider's terms (cost
   ceiling, launch date, risk tolerance). These become the comparison fields.
3. **Lay out the real options**, including "do nothing" or "decide later" when those are
   real, with the cost of waiting.
4. **Compare on identical fields** in one table. Each cell carries its basis: a source,
   or observed / inferred / unverified. Unknowns are shown as unknowns.
5. **Recommend**, with the two or three reasons that decide it, and state:
   - what would change the recommendation (the evidence that would flip it);
   - the main risk of the recommended option and how it is contained;
   - what is reversible and what isn't.
6. **Make the ask explicit**: exactly what the decider needs to say yes or no to, and
   what happens next in each case.
7. **Keep the decider in charge.** Present trade-offs honestly, including the best case
   for the option you didn't recommend. Don't hide an option to steer the choice.

## Shape

```
Decision: <what>, by <whom>, by <date>
Recommendation: <option> — because <reason 1>, <reason 2>
Would change if: <evidence>
Ask: <the yes/no or choice needed>

| Field | Option A | Option B | Do nothing |
|---|---|---|---|

Risks and reversibility · What happens next · Sources
```

## Quality bar

- The first screen alone is enough to decide.
- Every option is described as its strongest advocate would accept.
- No figure in the table is without its basis.
- Budget: one page, plus an appendix only if the decider asked for detail.
